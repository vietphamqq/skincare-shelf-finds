You get off a long flight and your skin feels like it belongs to someone else: tight across the cheeks, flaky at the edges, lips cracked, eyes gritty. It isn't your imagination and it isn't really about the flight itself. It's about the air.

Aircraft cabins are pressurized with air drawn from outside at altitude, where there's almost no moisture to begin with. The result is an environment substantially drier than the indoor air you're used to, and you sit in it for hours. Dry air pulls water out of your skin, and the longer the exposure, the more noticeable it gets.

The good news is that the fix is boring, cheap, and fits in a clear plastic bag.

## Before you fly

**Go with a clean, bare face where you can.** You don't have to fly without makeup, but a bare face makes it far easier to reapply moisture during the flight, and there's less sitting on the skin for hours. If you'd rather not, at least skip a heavy base.

**Moisturize before you board, not after takeoff.** Applying a good layer while you're still in normal humidity gives it something to hold onto. Apply to slightly damp skin if you can manage it — after washing your hands and face at the gate works fine.

**Pack the small stuff.** A lip balm, a hand cream, and a travel-size moisturizer cover most of it. Add eye drops if your eyes dry out, and a sheet mask if you're on a genuinely long flight and don't mind being seen in one.

**Skip introducing a new active.** A plane is a bad place to discover you react to something. Whatever you're trialling can wait until you land.

## During the flight

**Reapply moisturizer every few hours.** More often than you think you need to. This is the whole strategy.

**Balm on lips repeatedly.** Lips have no oil glands of their own and they go first.

**A sheet mask once the cabin lights go down** is genuinely useful on a long-haul overnight leg, mostly because it holds a layer of hydrating fluid against skin for fifteen or twenty minutes. Pat in what's left rather than rinsing.

**Drink water and go easy on the alcohol and coffee.** Staying hydrated matters for how you feel generally, and cabin dryness affects your nose, throat and eyes as much as your skin.

**Don't skip sunscreen on a daytime flight if you're in a window seat.** You're closer to the sun and aircraft windows don't block UVA the way you might assume. If you're sitting by the window in daylight for hours, keep your usual SPF on and reapply, or close the shade.

**Leave your face alone otherwise.** Tray tables and armrests are among the least clean surfaces you'll touch all week, and touching your face after them is a good way to arrive with a breakout that has nothing to do with the air.

## When you land

Wash your face with a gentle cleanser, apply moisturizer to damp skin, and go back to your normal routine. If skin feels sensitized, hold off on actives for a day or two rather than trying to "fix" it with an exfoliant, which is the most common instinct and the least helpful one.

Dry indoor air at your destination — hotel heating, air conditioning — can extend the problem past the flight itself. A travel humidifier is overkill for most trips, but a bowl of water near a radiator does a surprising amount, and so does not cranking the air conditioning to its lowest setting overnight.

## When to check in with a professional

Post-flight dryness settles within a day or two of normal care. What isn't ordinary is skin that stays red, itchy, cracked or painful for longer, a rash that appears and spreads, or persistent swelling. Frequent flyers who find their skin never quite recovers between trips, or anyone with eczema or rosacea that flares badly with travel, would get more out of a conversation with a board-certified dermatologist than out of another product.

## The short version

- Cabin air is much drier than normal indoor air, which is what pulls moisture out of your skin on a long flight.
- Board with a clean face and moisturizer already applied; reapply every few hours in the air.
- Lip balm, hand cream and a travel-size moisturizer cover most of it; a sheet mask helps on overnight legs.
- Keep sunscreen on for daytime window seats and reapply, or close the shade.
- Drink water, go easy on alcohol and coffee, and avoid touching your face after tray tables.
- Don't start a new active before or during a trip.
- Persistent redness, cracking, itching or a spreading rash is worth a board-certified dermatologist's time.

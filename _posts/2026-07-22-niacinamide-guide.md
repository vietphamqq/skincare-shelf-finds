Niacinamide is a form of vitamin B3 that shows up in everything from serums to moisturizers, and its reputation as a do-a-little-of-everything ingredient is mostly earned. It's commonly used to help the look of enlarged pores and uneven tone and to support a comfortable, healthy-looking moisture barrier — and unlike many actives, it's gentle enough that almost anyone can slot it into a routine without drama.

## What niacinamide is

Niacinamide (sometimes called nicotinamide) is a water-soluble form of vitamin B3. In skincare formulas it's prized for being stable, well-tolerated, and easy to formulate — which is exactly why you'll find it in cleansers, toners, serums, moisturizers, and sunscreens alike.

It's not an exfoliant, it's not a retinoid, and it doesn't come with the adjustment period those ingredients are known for. Think of it as a steady supporting player rather than a dramatic headliner.

## What it's commonly used for

No single ingredient does everything, but niacinamide is associated with an unusually broad set of cosmetic benefits. It's commonly used to:

- **Help the look of enlarged pores.** Many people find that with consistent use over weeks, pores appear less prominent and skin looks smoother overall.
- **Even out the look of skin tone.** It's a frequent pick in products aimed at dullness and the appearance of discoloration, and many people find their skin looks brighter and more uniform over time.
- **Support the moisture barrier.** Niacinamide is often included in barrier-focused formulas, where it can help skin feel more comfortable, look less flaky, and hold onto hydration better.
- **Balance the look of oily skin.** People with shine-prone skin often reach for it because it feels lightweight and many find their skin looks less slick through the day.

Notice the theme: gradual, cumulative, cosmetic-level improvements. Niacinamide rewards consistency over weeks and months, not overnight.

## Percentages: why more isn't better

You'll commonly see niacinamide on labels at concentrations like 2%, 5%, or 10% — and a persistent myth says higher must be better. In practice, the opposite caution applies.

Lower and mid-range concentrations — think the 5% neighborhood — are widely considered the sweet spot where most people get the benefits with minimal fuss. At 10% and above, some people notice flushing, tingling, or general irritation, without a clear payoff for the extra strength.

So if you're choosing between a modest percentage and a maximal one, the modest one is the smarter default — especially if your skin leans sensitive. Many excellent moisturizers don't disclose a percentage at all, and that's fine too: formulation matters more than the number on the front of the bottle.

## Where it fits in your routine

Niacinamide is refreshingly low-maintenance about placement:

- **AM or PM — or both.** It has no sun-sensitivity baggage, so it works at any time of day.
- **Serum step:** apply after cleansing and before moisturizer.
- **In a moisturizer:** just use it as your regular moisturizer step. This is the easiest entry point of all.
- **Frequency:** daily use is typical. No ramp-up ladder required, though introducing any new product gradually is never a bad idea.

You genuinely don't need a dedicated niacinamide serum to benefit. If your moisturizer or sunscreen already contains it, you're covered — adding two or three more niacinamide products on top mostly just adds cost, and stacking several high-percentage products is one of the few ways people manage to get irritated by this otherwise gentle ingredient.

As always with a new active: patch test first on a small area for a few days before applying it to your whole face.

## Who it suits

Niacinamide's tolerability is its superpower, so the honest answer is: most people.

- **Oily and blemish-prone skin** often loves its lightweight feel and the way it can help the look of shine and pores.
- **Dry or barrier-stressed skin** benefits from its place in soothing, moisture-supporting formulas.
- **Sensitive skin** generally tolerates it well at modest percentages — it's one of the safer actives to experiment with.
- **Retinoid and acid users** often add it precisely because it plays nicely alongside stronger ingredients and can make a routine feel more comfortable overall.

If you're not sure what your skin leans toward, our [skin-types]({{ '/skin-types/' | relative_url }}) guides can help you read the signs. And if you have persistent skin concerns — irritation that won't settle, breakouts that don't budge, discoloration you're worried about — see a board-certified dermatologist rather than trying to solve it with more products.

## Pairing-friendliness: niacinamide gets along with everyone

If skincare ingredients had a yearbook, niacinamide would be voted easiest to get along with. It layers comfortably with:

- **Retinoids** — a popular pairing; many people find niacinamide makes retinol nights feel less drying.
- **Exfoliating acids (AHA/BHA)** — generally fine in the same routine, since niacinamide adds comfort rather than extra exfoliation.
- **Vitamin C** — despite an old rumor that the two cancel each other out, that concern traces back to decades-old lab conditions and is widely considered outdated for modern formulations. Plenty of current products combine them on purpose.
- **Hyaluronic acid, ceramides, peptides, sunscreen** — no conflicts; these are natural teammates.

The only realistic caution: if your skin flushes or stings when you combine a high-percentage niacinamide product with other strong actives in one sitting, simplify. Drop to a lower percentage or move things to different times of day.

## The short version

- Niacinamide is a gentle, stable form of vitamin B3 found in every product category.
- It's commonly used to help the look of pores, uneven tone, and shine, and to support the moisture barrier.
- Around 5% is plenty for most people; 10%+ raises the odds of irritation without a clear extra payoff.
- Use it AM or PM, daily, as a serum or simply inside your moisturizer — one product with it is enough.
- It pairs comfortably with retinoids, acids, vitamin C, and basically everything else.
- Patch test new products, and see a board-certified dermatologist for persistent skin concerns.

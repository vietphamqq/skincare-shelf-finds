Taking off makeup is the step people do when they're tired, in a hurry, and least inclined to be careful. It's also the step where most of the damage happens — not from the products, but from the rubbing.

Skin around the eyes is thin and moves easily, and a nightly routine of dragging a dry wipe across it adds up. The good news is that the gentler method is barely slower once you know the order.

## Dissolve first, wipe second

The principle that makes everything else easier: makeup is largely oil-based, and oil dissolves oil. Water-based cleansers struggle with foundation, waterproof mascara and sunscreen, which is why people compensate by scrubbing.

Start with an oil cleanser, a cleansing balm, or micellar water, applied to **dry** skin. Massage gently for twenty or thirty seconds and let the product do the lifting. When makeup has actually dissolved, it comes away with almost no pressure.

Then follow with your usual gentle cleanser and lukewarm water to remove the residue. That's the whole of "double cleansing," and this — a day you've worn makeup or sunscreen — is exactly when it earns its place.

## The eye area, specifically

This is where technique matters most.

Soak a cotton pad, hold it against closed eyes for five to ten seconds, and let the makeup loosen before you wipe. Then wipe once, downward and outward, rather than scrubbing back and forth. Repeat with a fresh pad if needed. Waterproof formulas need longer soaking, not more force.

Use a product intended for the eye area, and keep in mind that anything used near the eyes carries a bit more risk of irritation and infection. Regulators are specific about eye cosmetics: don't share them, replace mascara regularly, never add water or saliva to a dried-out tube, and stop using anything that causes irritation. If you've had an eye infection, throw out what you were using at the time.

Contact lens wearers: take lenses out before you start.

## What to be wary of

**Dry wipes as your only method.** Makeup wipes are convenient for travel and better than nothing after a long day, but they work largely through friction, and they leave residue behind. If you use them, follow with a proper cleanse.

**Scrubbing anything.** No cleanser needs pressure to work. If you find yourself pressing hard, the product isn't right for the job.

**Hot water.** It doesn't dissolve makeup better and it does strip skin.

**Skipping it entirely.** Sleeping in makeup occasionally isn't a catastrophe, but as a habit it leaves the day's oil, sweat and product sitting on skin overnight, and eye makeup left on is a common cause of irritation.

## After you've finished

Pat dry rather than rubbing, and apply moisturizer while skin is still slightly damp. If you use any treatment products at night, this is when they go on — on clean skin, not over a layer of half-removed foundation.

One more habit worth having: wash your makeup brushes. Dermatology guidance suggests cleaning them regularly, since brushes and sponges collect oil, product and bacteria, and a dirty brush undoes some of the care you just took.

## When to check in with a professional

Redness, itching or swelling around the eyes that doesn't settle after you stop using a product, styes or recurring eye irritation, or a rash that appears after a new makeup or remover, are all worth having looked at rather than working around. See a board-certified dermatologist for persistent skin reactions, and an eye care professional for anything affecting the eye itself.

## The short version

- Dissolve makeup with an oil cleanser, balm or micellar water on dry skin, then follow with your usual gentle cleanser.
- Friction, not product, causes most makeup-removal irritation.
- For eyes: soak a pad, hold five to ten seconds, wipe once downward — don't scrub, and give waterproof formulas longer.
- Don't share eye makeup, never add water to a dried tube, and toss anything used during an eye infection.
- Dry wipes are a backup, not a method; follow them with a real cleanse.
- Pat dry and moisturize while damp; wash makeup brushes regularly.
- Persistent redness, itching or swelling deserves a board-certified dermatologist's input.

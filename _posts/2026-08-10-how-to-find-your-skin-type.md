Almost every skincare article eventually says "choose a product suited to your skin type," and then moves on as if everyone already knows theirs. Plenty of people don't, or they're working from an assumption they made in their teens and never revisited. Skin changes with age, climate, seasons and what you've been putting on it, so the answer you had at nineteen may not be the answer now.

The good news is that you don't need a device, an app, or a consultation to find out. There's a simple bare-face test, it takes about an hour, and the only thing it costs is not putting anything on your face for a while.

## The bare-face test

Wash your face with a gentle cleanser, pat it dry, and then apply nothing at all. No toner, no serum, no moisturizer, no makeup. Leave it completely bare and go do something else for around an hour.

Then look in a mirror in decent light and pay attention to two things: how your skin feels, and where it shines.

**Tight, rough or flaky across the whole face** points to dry skin. Dry skin produces less oil than it needs and tends to feel uncomfortable rather than just look a certain way.

**Shine everywhere, including the cheeks**, points to oily skin, which produces more sebum than it needs. Pores are often more visible, and skin can feel slick to the touch by mid-morning.

**Shine along the forehead, nose and chin but normal or dry cheeks** is combination skin, easily the most common result. The T-zone has more oil glands than the rest of the face, so a bit of shine there is completely normal.

**Comfortable, neither tight nor greasy, with no particular complaints** is what usually gets called normal or balanced skin.

**Sensitive** is a slightly different category. It's less about oil and more about reactivity: skin that stings, burns, itches or turns red easily in response to products, fragrance, heat or weather. You can have dry sensitive skin or oily sensitive skin, so treat it as an extra label rather than a replacement for one of the four above.

If you want a second opinion, the blotting-sheet version works too: press a clean blotting paper or tissue to different areas an hour after cleansing and see where it picks up oil. Lots of oil everywhere suggests oily, oil only from the T-zone suggests combination, almost none suggests dry.

## Do the test on a normal day

A few things will skew the result, so it's worth choosing your moment.

Don't test the morning after using a strong exfoliant or a retinoid, since freshly irritated skin will read as drier and more sensitive than it usually is. Don't test straight after a hot shower or a workout. And try to avoid testing in unusual conditions, like the day you arrive somewhere much drier or much more humid than home.

Doing the test once in summer and once in winter is genuinely useful. Many people are combination in July and dry in January, and it's not a contradiction, it's just seasonal.

## What your result actually changes

Knowing your skin type is useful, but it changes less than the marketing implies. The core routine is the same for everyone: a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer, and daily broad-spectrum sunscreen. What your type mostly influences is texture and emphasis.

**Dry skin** does better with creamier cleansers and richer moisturizers, applied while skin is still slightly damp. Long hot showers and foaming cleansers that leave a squeaky feeling are the usual culprits when dryness gets worse.

**Oily skin** still needs moisturizer, which surprises people. Stripping oil away aggressively often backfires. Lighter gel textures and products labelled non-comedogenic are a reasonable starting point.

**Combination skin** rarely needs two separate routines. Most people do fine with one lightweight moisturizer, applying a little more where it's dry and a little less on the T-zone.

**Sensitive skin** benefits most from what you leave out: fragrance-free formulas, fewer actives at once, and patch testing anything new before it goes on your whole face.

**Normal skin** mostly needs consistency and sunscreen, which is a less exciting answer than the industry would like.

Whatever the result, introduce new products one at a time. If you change four things at once and your skin reacts, you've learned nothing about which one caused it.

## When to check in with a professional

The bare-face test tells you about oiliness and comfort. It doesn't diagnose anything. If what you're dealing with is persistent redness, flaking that won't settle, itching, or breakouts that don't respond to a simple gentle routine, that may be a skin condition rather than a skin type, and a board-certified dermatologist can tell the difference far faster than trial and error with products can.

## The short version

- Wash with a gentle cleanser, apply nothing for about an hour, then check how skin feels and where it shines.
- Tight all over means dry; shine everywhere means oily; oily T-zone with normal or dry cheeks means combination; comfortable and uncomplaining means normal.
- Sensitive is an add-on label about reactivity, not a fifth alternative — you can be dry and sensitive, or oily and sensitive.
- Don't test right after exfoliating, a hot shower, or a big change of climate.
- Retest seasonally; being combination in summer and dry in winter is normal.
- Everyone's core routine is the same — cleanser, moisturizer, daily SPF — your type mainly changes textures and emphasis.
- Persistent redness, itching, flaking or breakouts is a reason to see a board-certified dermatologist.

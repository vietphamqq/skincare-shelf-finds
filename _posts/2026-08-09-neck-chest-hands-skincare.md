Think about where your skincare actually lands. Cleanser goes on your face. Serum goes on your face. Moisturizer goes on your face, and sunscreen, if you wear it, usually stops somewhere around the jawline. Meanwhile your neck, chest and hands are out in the same weather, the same central heating, and the same daylight, with none of the attention.

You don't need a separate product line for any of them. Almost all of this is about extending what you already own by a few centimetres and adding one habit at the sink. Here's the short version of what actually matters, and why these areas behave a little differently from your face.

## Why these areas show wear sooner

The skin on the neck and chest is thinner than the skin on your cheeks, with fewer oil glands to keep it comfortable. That means it dries out more readily and takes longer to bounce back from irritation. The chest in particular spends years in open collars and V-necks catching sun that the face, with its hats and its sunscreen, is often spared.

Hands are a different story. They're exposed constantly, and unlike the rest of you they get washed several times a day, every day. Frequent handwashing is genuinely good for you, but soap and water strip away the oils that keep the skin's outer layer intact, which is exactly why hands go tight, rough and cracked so much faster than anywhere else. Dermatology guidance on handwashing is blunt about the fix: moisturize right after washing, while the skin is still damp.

None of this is a crisis. It's just a case of these areas doing more work with less support.

## Extending your face routine downward

The simplest change is the one that costs nothing.

**Bring cleanser past the jawline.** Not a scrub, just the same gentle cleanser you already use, carried down the neck. If you wear sunscreen on your neck, which you should, it needs to come off in the evening the same way it does on your face.

**Take moisturizer and serum down to the neck and chest.** When you've finished with your face, use whatever's left on your fingers on the neck, working upward with light pressure rather than dragging down. If you use a serum, the same applies. You don't need a dedicated neck cream. A "neck cream" is generally a moisturizer with different packaging, and if your current moisturizer suits your skin, it will suit your neck.

**Go easy with actives here.** Retinoids and exfoliating acids can be used on the neck and chest, but because the skin is thinner, it tends to protest sooner. Start at a lower frequency than you would on your face, once or twice a week, and use less product than instinct suggests. If it stings or flakes, back off rather than pushing through.

**Sunscreen is the one that matters most.** Broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, applied generously to face, neck, ears, chest and the backs of the hands, is the single highest-value habit in this entire article. Regulators and dermatology bodies are consistent on the basics: apply it about fifteen minutes before going out, reapply at least every two hours, and reapply after swimming or heavy sweating. The backs of the hands are one of the most commonly forgotten spots, and they're also one of the most consistently exposed.

## A realistic approach to hands

Hands need less strategy and more repetition.

**Moisturize after every wash, while damp.** This is the whole game. Keep a hand cream by the kitchen sink, one in your bag, one at your desk. The barrier to doing this is never motivation, it's whether a tube is within arm's reach at the moment your hands are still wet.

**Choose something thicker than you'd use on your face.** Creams and ointments hold moisture in better than thin lotions, and hands tolerate a richer texture happily. Fragrance-free is a sensible default if your hands are already irritated.

**Use lukewarm water, not hot.** Hot water feels like it's cleaning better. It isn't; it's just stripping more oil.

**Wear gloves for wet or messy work.** Washing up, cleaning products, gardening. Gloves are unglamorous and they work.

**Don't forget sunscreen on the backs of your hands**, particularly if you drive a lot or spend time outdoors. Reapply after washing, since that's effectively removing it.

**Look after the nail folds too.** The skin around the nails cracks easily in dry weather, and a dab of the same cream or a plain ointment is usually enough.

## What to expect, and what not to

It's worth being straightforward about this: a consistent routine on the neck, chest and hands keeps skin more comfortable, better hydrated, and better protected from further sun exposure. That's a real benefit and it compounds quietly over years.

What it isn't is a reversal button. Products applied to the surface don't undo sun damage that's already happened, and anyone promising that in a jar is selling you something. The most effective thing on this list is still sunscreen, precisely because it's about what doesn't happen next.

## When to check in with a professional

Hands are the area most likely to need more than a routine. If your hands are dry, scaly, itchy or cracked in a way that moisturizer isn't touching, if the skin splits or bleeds, or if the problem keeps coming back, that may be hand eczema or a contact reaction rather than ordinary dryness, and it's worth seeing a board-certified dermatologist rather than cycling through more creams. The same applies to any new, changing or non-healing spot on the chest, neck or backs of the hands, all of which are common sites for sun-related skin changes and worth having looked at promptly.

## The short version

- Neck and chest skin is thinner with fewer oil glands, so it dries out and irritates more easily than your face.
- You don't need dedicated products: carry your existing cleanser, serum and moisturizer past the jawline.
- Use actives more sparingly on the neck and chest than on the face, and stop if there's stinging or flaking.
- Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on face, neck, ears, chest and the backs of the hands is the highest-value habit here; reapply about every two hours outdoors.
- For hands, moisturize after every wash while skin is still damp, use lukewarm water, and wear gloves for wet work.
- Surface products help with comfort and protection; they don't reverse existing sun damage.
- Cracked, itchy or persistently dry hands, or any new or changing spot, is a reason to see a board-certified dermatologist.

Most skincare aimed at men falls into one of two camps. Either it's a ten-step regimen borrowed wholesale from somewhere else, or it's a bar of soap and whatever's in the shower. The useful version sits between the two, and it's smaller than you'd expect: three products, about a minute in the morning and a minute at night.

Here's what those three are, why each earns its place, and what to do about the one thing that genuinely is different when you shave.

## The three that matter

**A gentle cleanser, morning and night.** Body soap and bar soaps are formulated for skin that's tougher than your face, and using them there is one of the most common reasons skin ends up tight and irritated. A mild face cleanser rinses off sweat, oil and the day without stripping. Warm water rather than hot, fingertips rather than a scrubbing pad, and that's the whole step.

**A moisturizer, applied while skin is still damp.** This is the step people skip because they assume moisturizer is for dry skin only. It isn't. Skin that's been cleansed has lost some of its surface moisture, and putting something back within a couple of minutes is what keeps it comfortable. If your skin runs oily, a light lotion or gel does the job; if it runs dry, something creamier. Fragrance-free is a safe default.

**A broad-spectrum sunscreen every morning.** If you only adopt one thing on this page, make it this one. Dermatology guidance is consistent: broad-spectrum, SPF 30 or higher, water resistant, applied to face, neck, ears and any exposed skin, reapplied roughly every two hours outdoors and after swimming or heavy sweating. It matters year-round, not just in summer, and it matters more if you're outdoors for work or sport. Men are diagnosed with skin cancer at high rates and are, on average, less likely to use sun protection consistently, which makes this the highest-value habit available.

That's the routine. Cleanser, moisturizer, sunscreen. Everything else is optional.

## Shaving, which is the part that's actually different

Shaving is exfoliation whether you intend it or not, and it's where most shaving-related skin trouble comes from.

The standard advice from dermatologists is worth following closely. Wet the skin and hair first, ideally after a shower when hair is softened, since shaving dry hair is rough on both the blade and the skin. Use a shaving cream or gel rather than dry-shaving or using plain soap. Shave in the direction the hair grows, not against it. Use short, light strokes and rinse the blade after each one. Change the blade regularly — a dull blade drags, and dragging is what causes irritation. Store the razor somewhere it can dry out rather than in the damp of the shower.

Afterwards, skip the traditional alcohol-heavy splash if your skin objects to it. That sting isn't a sign it's working; it's a sign it's drying. A fragrance-free moisturizer or a post-shave balm does more good.

If you get razor bumps — small, sometimes painful bumps where hairs curl back into the skin — shaving less often, using a single-blade or electric razor set slightly away from the skin, and not stretching the skin taut while shaving all tend to help. This is more common in people with curly or coarse facial hair, and it's a specific condition rather than a technique failure, so it's worth reading dermatology guidance on it or seeing someone if it persists.

## What you can ignore, at least for now

Toner isn't essential. A separate eye cream is optional if your moisturizer is gentle enough to use around the eyes. Scrubs with rough particles are easy to overdo, especially on skin that's already being exfoliated by a razor several times a week. Anything promising dramatic transformation is selling.

If, after a few months of the basics, you want to add something for a specific concern — a retinoid for texture and fine lines, a vitamin C serum for tone, a salicylic acid product for congestion — add one at a time, use it a couple of times a week to start, and give it several weeks before judging. Adding three at once and reacting badly teaches you nothing about which one caused it.

## When to check in with a professional

A simple routine handles everyday maintenance. It doesn't handle everything. Persistent acne, ingrown hairs that keep getting infected, a rash that won't settle, or any new, changing, bleeding or non-healing spot on your skin is a reason to see a board-certified dermatologist rather than wait. Skin checks are quick, and skin cancer found early is far more treatable than skin cancer found late.

## The short version

- Three products cover it: a gentle face cleanser, a moisturizer applied to damp skin, and daily broad-spectrum SPF 30+.
- Don't wash your face with body or bar soap; it's formulated for tougher skin.
- Sunscreen is the highest-value habit here — reapply about every two hours outdoors.
- Shave after a shower on wet, softened hair, with cream or gel, in the direction of growth, using short strokes and a sharp blade.
- Replace the alcohol splash with a fragrance-free balm or moisturizer.
- Add actives one at a time, a few times a week, and give each several weeks.
- New, changing or non-healing spots, or persistent irritation, warrant a board-certified dermatologist.

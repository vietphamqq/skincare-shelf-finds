Face oils occupy a strange spot in skincare. They're marketed as luxurious and transformative, they're the step people most often add when they feel like their routine "isn't enough," and they're also the step most often used wrongly — too much, too early in the routine, or as a replacement for a moisturizer they should have kept.

The honest answer is that a face oil is a nice-to-have rather than an essential, and whether it earns a place on your shelf depends on what your skin actually needs.

## What an oil does, and what it doesn't

Moisturizing ingredients fall into three broad groups. Humectants, like glycerin and hyaluronic acid, draw water into the outer layer of skin. Emollients smooth and soften the surface. Occlusives sit on top and slow how fast water evaporates away.

Face oils work mostly in the second and third categories. They soften, and they help hold moisture in. What they generally do not do is add water to skin, which is the thing people assume when they reach for one to fix dryness. An oil on dehydrated skin with nothing underneath it is sealing in not very much.

That's the single most useful thing to understand: an oil is a lid, not a drink.

## Where it goes in the routine

Because oils are the heaviest, most occlusive thing in a typical routine, they go last — after your serum and after your moisturizer. Skincare is generally applied thinnest to thickest, and putting an oil before a water-based product means the water-based product has to get through it.

The practical version, for an evening routine: cleanse, treatment or serum, moisturizer, then oil if you're using one.

Two or three drops covers a face. Warm them between your palms and press the oil into skin rather than rubbing it around; it spreads further than you expect. If your face feels slick twenty minutes later, you used too much.

## Who tends to benefit

**Dry skin, particularly in winter or dry indoor air.** This is the clearest case. An oil over a moisturizer noticeably reduces that tight, thirsty feeling overnight.

**Skin that's temporarily stressed.** After a period of over-exfoliating, or during a stretch of harsh weather, a simple oil can be a comfortable last step while you simplify everything else.

**People who like the ritual.** Not a clinical reason, and a perfectly good one. A routine you enjoy is a routine you keep.

## Who can comfortably skip it

If your moisturizer already keeps skin comfortable all day, an oil adds cost and steps without adding much. Oily and acne-prone skin can use oils, but it's the group most likely to find them unnecessary or to react to heavier ones — coconut oil in particular is well known for clogging pores on some people. If you're breakout-prone and want to try one, patch test, pick a lighter option, and treat it as an experiment rather than an upgrade.

An oil is also not a substitute for sunscreen, and applying oil before sun exposure doesn't offer protection.

## Choosing one without overthinking it

Lighter options like squalane and jojoba tend to sit well on most skin and are a reasonable starting point. Rosehip and marula are common in the "richer" category. Fragrance-free is the safer default, and essential oils added for scent are a frequent cause of irritation, so a plainer formula is usually the kinder one.

Start with two nights a week rather than nightly. Give it a few weeks. If skin looks the same with and without, you have your answer and you've saved yourself a recurring purchase.

## When to check in with a professional

If a new oil brings on breakouts, redness, itching or a rash that doesn't settle after you stop, or if the dryness you were trying to fix keeps coming back no matter what you layer on, that's worth a conversation with a board-certified dermatologist rather than another bottle. Persistent dryness can have causes that no amount of oil addresses, and reactions are easier to pin down with help than by trial and error.

## The short version

- Face oils mainly soften and seal moisture in; they don't add water to skin.
- Apply last, after moisturizer, using two or three drops pressed in rather than rubbed.
- Best suited to dry skin, dry air, and temporarily stressed skin.
- Optional for everyone else; oily and acne-prone skin should patch test and go light (coconut oil clogs pores on some people).
- Squalane and jojoba are sensible starting points; fragrance-free is the safer default.
- Try two nights a week for a few weeks before deciding it earns its place.
- Reactions that don't settle, or dryness that keeps returning, are worth a board-certified dermatologist's input.

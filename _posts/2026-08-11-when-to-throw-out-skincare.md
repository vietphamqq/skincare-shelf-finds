Open the cabinet of almost anyone who likes skincare and you'll find at least one product that has been there a while. A half-used serum from two winters ago. A jar of cream bought on holiday. A sunscreen from a summer you can't quite place. None of them have an obvious use-by date on the front, which makes it genuinely hard to know whether they're still fine or quietly past it.

Here's what the packaging is actually telling you, and what to look for when it isn't telling you anything.

## Why most products don't have an expiry date

This surprises people: in the United States, cosmetics are not required to carry an expiration date. Manufacturers are responsible for making sure a product is safe, and shelf-life testing is part of that, but the regulator does not mandate a printed date the way it does for food or medicines. So the absence of a date on the bottle doesn't mean the product lasts forever. It means nobody was required to print one.

There's a second complication. The shelf life a manufacturer tests for assumes the product is stored and used in a reasonable way. Once you've opened it, dipped your fingers in it, left it on a sunny windowsill, or kept it in a steamy bathroom, you're outside those assumptions. Heat, light, air and repeated contact with skin and fingers all shorten the working life of a formula, and warm humid storage is exactly the kind of environment that encourages microbial growth.

## The little open-jar symbol

The most useful marking on most packaging is a small drawing of an open jar with a number and the letter M inside it: 6M, 12M, 24M. That's the period-after-opening, and it means the product is intended to be used within that many months once it's been opened for the first time.

It's a European labelling convention rather than a US requirement, but because brands sell across markets you'll find it on a great deal of what's on the shelf. It's worth actually looking for it: turn the box or the base of the bottle over, and it's usually near the recycling marks.

The catch is obvious once you say it out loud: the clock starts when you open it, and nothing on the packaging records that date. A small piece of masking tape with the month written on it, stuck to the bottom of the bottle, solves the problem permanently and costs nothing.

## The signs that matter more than any number

Dates and symbols are a guide. Your senses are the check.

**The color has shifted.** A vitamin C serum turning from clear or pale straw to deep orange or brown is the classic example. Oxidation doesn't necessarily make a product unsafe, but it does mean the ingredient you paid for has degraded.

**The smell has changed.** Anything that smells sour, metallic, or simply not how it smelled when you bought it has changed chemically. Trust this one.

**The texture has separated or gone grainy.** Creams that have split into oil and water, lotions that have gone lumpy, or balms that have turned gritty are past the point where the formula is doing what it was designed to do.

**It stings now and it didn't before.** A product you tolerated happily for months that suddenly bites is telling you something, whether it's the product that changed or your skin.

**Anything you can see growing in it.** Rare, but decisive. Throw it out.

Sunscreen deserves a specific mention because it's the one where degradation actually costs you protection. In the US, sunscreen is regulated as an over-the-counter drug rather than a cosmetic, so it does carry an expiry date. If yours has passed it, or you've kept it in a hot car all summer, replace it rather than gambling on it.

## Eye products are the strict category

Anything used near the eyes is the exception to a relaxed approach. The eye area is more vulnerable to irritation and infection, and applicators that go from eye to tube and back again carry whatever's on your lashes with them.

Standard guidance is to replace mascara every few months, never to add water or saliva to thin out a dried-out formula, never to share eye products, and to throw anything out immediately if you've had an eye infection. Wands that have been used while your eye was irritated should not go back into the tube.

## A few habits that buy you time

Close lids properly. Store products out of direct sunlight and away from the hottest, steamiest part of the bathroom. Use a small spatula or clean hands for anything in an open jar rather than repeatedly dipping fingers into it. Don't decant into a random container unless you need to, and if you do, use something clean and airtight.

Products in pumps and tubes generally last better than open jars simply because less air and fewer fingers get in.

And buy at a pace you'll actually use. A serum that expires half-used is more expensive than one that costs twice as much and gets finished.

## When to check in with a professional

If a product causes a reaction that doesn't settle after you stop using it, if you develop a rash, persistent redness or swelling, or if you have any eye irritation or infection after using a product near your eyes, that's a moment to get it looked at rather than wait it out. A board-certified dermatologist can also help work out whether you're reacting to a specific ingredient rather than an old formula, which is useful information for everything you buy afterwards.

## The short version

- Cosmetics in the US aren't required to carry expiry dates; sunscreen is regulated differently and does.
- The open-jar symbol (6M, 12M, 24M) is the period after opening — write the opening month on the bottle, since nothing else records it.
- Heat, light, air and fingers shorten a product's real-world life regardless of what the label says.
- Replace anything whose color, smell or texture has changed, or that suddenly stings.
- Eye products are the strict category: replace mascara often, never add water, never share, and toss after an eye infection.
- Pumps and tubes outlast open jars; store away from sun and bathroom steam.
- A reaction that doesn't settle, or any eye infection, is worth seeing a board-certified dermatologist about.

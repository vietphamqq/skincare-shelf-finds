Almost nobody patch tests. A new product arrives, it goes straight onto the face that evening, and if something goes wrong two days later there's no way to tell which of the four things you changed that week caused it.

Patch testing is the least exciting habit in skincare and one of the few that costs nothing at all. It takes a small amount of product, a spot of skin you don't mind, and about a week of patience.

## Why it's worth a week

Two things can go wrong with a new product, and they don't behave the same way.

The first is straightforward irritation: stinging, redness or dryness that shows up quickly and affects most people if the concentration is high enough. The second is an allergic reaction, where your immune system has decided it objects to a specific ingredient. That kind often doesn't appear on first contact — it can take days of repeated exposure to show up, which is exactly why a single dab and a five-minute wait tells you almost nothing.

Dermatology guidance on contact dermatitis is consistent on this point: reactions to cosmetics and skincare can develop after repeated use, sometimes to a product you've used happily for a while. A week of testing on a small patch is a much cheaper way to find out than a week of testing on your whole face.

## Where to test

The inner forearm is the standard spot. It's easy to reach, easy to keep an eye on, and out of the way if something does happen.

The skin behind the ear or along the jawline is closer in thickness and behaviour to facial skin, which makes it a better predictor for face products — at the cost of being more visible if a reaction shows up. Either is reasonable. Pick one and use the same spot throughout.

For a body product, test where you'd actually use it.

## How to do it

Apply a small amount — roughly the size you'd use on that area normally — to a patch about the size of a coin. Do this twice a day, in the same place, for seven to ten days. Leave it on the way you'd use the product: rinse-off products get rinsed after the usual contact time, leave-on products stay on.

Don't cover it with a bandage unless a product's own instructions say to. Occluding skin pushes more of the product in and can turn a mild formula into an irritating one, which tells you about the bandage rather than about the product.

The rule that makes the whole exercise work: **one new product at a time.** If you start two things the same week and something reacts, you've learned nothing except that you now have to stop both.

## What a reaction looks like

Watch for redness, itching, burning, stinging that doesn't settle within a minute or two, small bumps, swelling, or dryness and flaking in the test area. Any of these means stop, rinse the area with cool water, and leave it alone until it settles.

A brief tingle on application that fades is common with some actives and isn't the same thing as burning that persists. The distinction is whether it goes away.

If ten days pass with nothing, the product is very likely fine for you. That isn't an absolute guarantee — sensitisation can still develop later — but it's most of the risk removed for the price of a week.

## When it matters most

Patch testing is worth the effort for anything with an active ingredient (retinoids, acids, vitamin C, benzoyl peroxide), anything scented, anything you'll use near the eyes, and any product going onto skin that's already sensitive, reactive, or recovering.

It matters less for a plain fragrance-free moisturizer from a brand you've used for years, though the habit is cheap enough that there's no real argument against it.

## When to check in with a professional

A reaction that spreads beyond the test area, blisters, swells noticeably, or doesn't settle within a few days after stopping the product is worth showing to a board-certified dermatologist rather than waiting out. The same goes for repeated reactions to products that seem unrelated — that pattern often points to a shared ingredient, and dermatologists can run proper patch testing to identify which one. Any reaction involving difficulty breathing or swelling of the face, lips or throat is an emergency and needs immediate medical care.

## The short version

- Patch test because allergic reactions often take days of repeated exposure to appear, not minutes.
- Use the inner forearm, or behind the ear and along the jawline for a closer match to facial skin.
- Apply a normal amount to a coin-sized patch, twice a day, for seven to ten days, in the same spot.
- Test only one new product at a time, or a reaction tells you nothing useful.
- Stop at redness, itching, burning, bumps or flaking; a brief tingle that fades is different.
- Prioritise testing for actives, fragranced products, anything used near the eyes, and sensitive or recovering skin.
- Reactions that spread, blister or persist after stopping deserve a board-certified dermatologist's opinion.

Starting retinol doesn't have to mean weeks of flaky, angry skin. The whole trick is going low and slow: a pea-sized amount, one or two nights a week to begin, plenty of moisturizer, and sunscreen every single morning. Here's exactly how to do it.

## What retinol actually is

Retinol is a form of vitamin A, part of a family of ingredients called retinoids. In skincare, it's commonly used to help improve the look of fine lines, uneven texture, and dullness, and many people find it helps their skin appear smoother and more even-toned over time.

The catch is that retinol is famous for a rocky adjustment period. Skin that isn't used to it can get dry, flaky, red, or sensitive — especially if you start with too much, too often. None of that means retinol isn't for you. It usually just means the introduction was rushed.

Before your first full application, do a patch test: apply a small amount to a discreet area (like behind the ear or on the inner forearm) for a few nights and watch for any reaction.

## Start low and slow: the frequency ladder

The gentlest way in is to climb a simple ladder, moving up only when your skin is comfortable at the current step:

1. **Weeks 1–2:** Apply retinol 1–2 nights per week, with several rest nights in between.
2. **Weeks 3–4:** If your skin feels calm, move to every third night.
3. **Weeks 5–8:** Try every other night.
4. **After that:** Some people eventually use it most nights; plenty of others stay at every other night indefinitely. More frequent isn't automatically better — consistency over months matters far more than nightly use.

If at any step your skin gets flaky or tender, drop back down a rung for a week or two. That's not failure; that's the system working.

It also helps to start with a lower-strength retinol product rather than the strongest one you can find. Gentle and consistent beats strong and sporadic.

## The sandwich method

If your skin leans dry or sensitive, "sandwiching" is a beginner favorite:

1. Cleanse, then apply a layer of fragrance-free moisturizer to fully dry skin.
2. Wait a few minutes, then apply your pea-sized amount of retinol.
3. Finish with another layer of moisturizer on top.

The moisturizer layers act as a buffer, slowing how quickly the retinol absorbs. Many people find this takes the edge off dryness and stinging while they adjust. Once your skin is comfortable, you can drop to just moisturizing after the retinol if you prefer.

One more note: apply retinol to dry skin. Damp skin can increase penetration, which sounds good but often just means more irritation for a beginner.

## How much to use: the pea rule

A pea-sized amount is enough for your entire face. Truly. Using more doesn't speed up results — it mostly speeds up irritation.

Dot it on your forehead, cheeks, and chin, then smooth it in. Steer around the immediate eye area, the corners of the nose, and the corners of the mouth, where skin is thinnest and irritation shows up first. A thin layer of plain moisturizer on those spots beforehand adds extra protection.

## Moisturizer and SPF are part of the deal

Think of retinol as one-third of a trio. The other two-thirds:

- **A fragrance-free moisturizer**, morning and night. Retinol can be drying while your skin adapts, and keeping your moisture barrier happy makes the whole process smoother.
- **A broad-spectrum SPF 30+ every morning — non-negotiable.** Skin using retinoids can be more sensitive to the sun, and daily sunscreen protects the very improvements you're working toward. If you're not ready to commit to daily SPF, you're not ready for retinol yet.

## Purging vs. irritation: how to tell the difference

Some people notice a temporary uptick in small breakouts during the first several weeks of retinol use — often called "purging." The common thinking is that this shows up in the areas where you usually break out, looks like your typical blemishes, and gradually settles as your skin adjusts.

Irritation looks different: redness, stinging, burning, flaking, or breakouts appearing in places you never break out. If that's what you're seeing, scale back the frequency, lean on the sandwich method, or pause entirely until your skin calms down.

If discomfort is significant or just won't quit, stop and reassess — and for persistent skin concerns, see a board-certified dermatologist. They can tell you far more looking at your actual skin than any article can.

## What not to combine on the same night

While you're adjusting, keep retinol nights simple. Commonly cited combinations to avoid in the same sitting:

- **Exfoliating acids (AHAs like glycolic or lactic acid, BHAs like salicylic acid).** Layering exfoliants with retinol is a classic recipe for irritation. Use them on alternate nights instead.
- **Benzoyl peroxide.** Traditionally kept separate from retinol; many people use one in the morning and the other at night.
- **Physical scrubs or peel products.** Same logic — too much at once.
- **Strong vitamin C products.** Not dangerous, but many people find splitting them (vitamin C in the morning, retinol at night) is more comfortable and simpler.

Friendly pairings, on the other hand: hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, ceramides, and plain moisturizers all tend to play nicely alongside retinol.

## The short version

- Retinol is commonly used to improve the look of fine lines, texture, and tone — but it demands a slow introduction.
- Start 1–2 nights per week with a pea-sized amount on dry skin, and climb the frequency ladder only when your skin is comfortable.
- The sandwich method (moisturizer, retinol, moisturizer) buffers dryness for sensitive skin.
- Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ every morning is non-negotiable while using retinoids.
- Keep exfoliating acids, benzoyl peroxide, and scrubs off your retinol nights — alternate instead.
- Patch test first, and see a board-certified dermatologist for persistent concerns.

There's a particular kind of skincare panic that arrives about five days before a wedding, an interview, a birthday or a photograph you'll have to live with. It usually leads to buying something new, doing an intensive mask, or booking a facial at a place you've never been.

Nearly all of that raises the risk of the exact thing you're trying to avoid. The useful version of event prep is mostly about *not* doing things.

## A realistic timeline

**Four to six weeks out.** This is the only window where starting something new makes sense. If you want to introduce an active or change your moisturizer, do it now, one product at a time, so there's time to see how skin responds and to recover if it doesn't. It's also the sensible window for any professional treatment you've had before — not one you haven't.

**Two weeks out.** Stop introducing anything new. From here your job is consistency: the routine you already know works, done properly, every day.

**One week out.** Ease off strong exfoliants — scrubs, high-strength acids, anything that leaves skin looking polished and feeling raw. Skin that's been over-exfoliated photographs as redness and takes days to settle. Keep sunscreen going; turning up sunburnt is a much more common problem than turning up dull.

**The night before.** Cleanse gently, moisturize while skin is slightly damp, and go to bed at a reasonable hour. This is not the night for a peel, a strong mask, or extractions in front of a mirror.

**The morning of.** Your normal routine, nothing new. Give moisturizer a few minutes to absorb before makeup so it doesn't pill, and don't skip sunscreen if you'll be outside.

## The mistakes that cause last-minute problems

**Trying a new product in the final week.** Reactions to cosmetics are common, and allergic reactions in particular can take days of repeated use to appear — which means a product introduced on Monday can show up on Friday.

**Booking a first-time facial or peel close to the date.** Even a good treatment can leave skin red, flaky or purging for several days, and you have no baseline for how yours will respond.

**Picking at a spot.** Squeezing pushes some of the contents deeper, tends to make the area more inflamed, and raises the odds of a mark that lasts far longer than the spot would have. A hydrocolloid patch overnight is the better move.

**Over-cleansing or over-masking.** Doing your routine three times as hard for a week doesn't compress weeks of progress into days; it mostly irritates.

**Forgetting the neck and chest.** If the outfit shows them, they need the same moisturizer and sunscreen the face gets.

## What actually helps in the short term

Sleep, water, a gentle routine done consistently, and lip balm — chapped lips are more visible in photographs than almost anything else. A plain hydrating moisturizer the night before makes skin look smoother the next morning in a way no emergency treatment does.

If you want one small addition, a sheet mask you've used before and know you tolerate is low-risk. A new one, from a brand you've never tried, three days beforehand, is not.

## When to check in with a professional

If a product reaction appears in the run-up — redness that spreads, swelling, a rash, or persistent stinging — stop using it and speak to a board-certified dermatologist rather than layering something else on top. The same applies if you're dealing with breakouts that are deep, painful or scarring, or a skin condition that flares before stressful events: both are more manageable with a plan made weeks ahead than with an emergency fix. Any reaction involving difficulty breathing or swelling of the face, lips or throat needs immediate medical care.

## The short version

- Four to six weeks out is the only sensible window for new products or first-time treatments.
- Stop introducing anything new two weeks before.
- Ease off strong exfoliants in the final week; keep sunscreen going.
- The night before: gentle cleanse, moisturize on damp skin, decent sleep. No peels, no extractions.
- Don't pick at spots — a hydrocolloid patch overnight is better.
- Include the neck and chest if they'll be visible, and don't neglect lip balm.
- Reactions in the run-up are worth a board-certified dermatologist's input rather than another product.
